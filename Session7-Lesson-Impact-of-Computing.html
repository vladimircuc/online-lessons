Impact of Computing

How computing affects society, culture, security, and ethics. This is 21-26% of the exam. It's all conceptual, no code tracing, but the vocabulary is dense and the questions can be tricky because they require nuanced thinking.

In this lesson: 1. Beneficial & Harmful Effects 2. The Digital Divide 3. Computing Bias 4. Crowdsourcing 5. Legal & Ethical: Copyright, Creative Commons, Open Source 6. PII & Data Privacy 7. Cybersecurity: Encryption, Phishing, Malware 8. Authentication & Cookies 9. End of Lesson Review (18 Questions)

1. Beneficial & Harmful Effects

Every computing innovation has BOTH beneficial and harmful effects. The exam will never ask if something is purely good or purely bad. It always asks you to consider both sides.

The Key Principle

Every computing innovation:
  ✓ Has intended benefits (what it was designed to do)
  ✓ Has unintended consequences (side effects nobody planned)
  ✓ Affects different groups differently
  ✓ Can be used in ways the creators didn't anticipate

Examples the Exam Loves

Social Media Benefits

Connects people globally

Enables free expression and organizing

Small businesses can reach customers

Rapid information sharing in emergencies

Social Media Concerns

Spreads misinformation rapidly

Cyberbullying and harassment

Addiction and mental health effects

Echo chambers and filter bubbles

Autonomous Vehicles Benefits

Could reduce human-error crashes

Mobility for elderly/disabled

More efficient traffic flow

Autonomous Vehicles Concerns

Job loss for drivers

Ethical decisions in crash scenarios

Software bugs could be fatal

Liability when accidents happen

Machine Learning Benefits

Medical diagnosis assistance

Personalized recommendations

Fraud detection

Machine Learning Concerns

Algorithmic bias from training data

Job displacement

Opaque decision-making ("black box")

Surveillance capabilities

Exam strategy: When asked about effects of a computing innovation, ALWAYS give both a benefit AND a concern. If you can only think of one side, you're missing something.

Quick Check: Effects

A ride-sharing app like Uber/Lyft. Give 2 benefits and 2 concerns.
Benefits: 1. Convenient, affordable transportation 2. Income opportunity for drivers Concerns: 1. Reduced income for traditional taxi drivers 2. Surge pricing exploits high-demand situations 3. Safety concerns (unvetted drivers) 4. Gig workers lack benefits (no insurance, etc.) 5. Collects extensive location/payment data

2. The Digital Divide

Definition

The digital divide is the gap between those who have access to computing technology and the internet and those who don't. It's not just about having a device, it's about meaningful, reliable access.

What Causes It

Economic:     Low-income families can't afford devices/internet
Geographic:   Rural areas lack broadband infrastructure
Age:          Elderly may lack digital literacy
Disability:   Technology not designed for accessibility
Global:       Developing countries have limited infrastructure
Education:    Schools in poor districts have fewer devices

Why It Matters

People without access miss out on:
  - Online education and job applications
  - Telehealth and government services
  - Communication and social connection
  - Economic opportunities (remote work, e-commerce)

The divide reinforces existing inequalities:
  Those already disadvantaged fall further behind.

Solutions (Exam Asks About These)

  - Public Wi-Fi and library computer access
  - Government subsidies for broadband in rural areas
  - Low-cost device programs for students
  - Accessible design (screen readers, captions, etc.)
  - Digital literacy training programs

Quick Check: Digital Divide

A school district moves all homework online. Which students are most affected by this decision, and why?
Students without reliable internet access or personal devices at home are most affected. This disproportionately impacts: - Low-income families - Rural students with no broadband - Students in crowded homes with shared devices Moving homework online assumes all students have equal access, which widens the digital divide rather than closing it. Solutions: provide loaner devices, offer offline alternatives, extend library hours.

3. Computing Bias

What Is Computing Bias?

Bias in computing happens when algorithms or systems systematically produce unfair results that favor or disadvantage certain groups.

Where Bias Comes From

1. Biased training data:
   If a hiring algorithm is trained on past hiring data
   where 90% of hires were men, it may learn to
   favor male applicants. The data reflects past bias,
   and the algorithm perpetuates it.

2. Biased programmers:
   Developers' unconscious assumptions get baked into
   the system. If all testers are young, they may not
   catch issues affecting elderly users.

3. Biased design choices:
   Facial recognition trained mostly on light-skinned
   faces performs worse on darker skin tones.
   The choice of training data IS a design choice.

4. Exclusion:
   If a dataset doesn't include certain groups at all,
   the algorithm won't work well for those groups.

Real-World Examples (Exam Loves These)

  - Facial recognition: higher error rates for
    women and people of color
  - Hiring algorithms: favoring male candidates
    based on historical hiring patterns
  - Predictive policing: over-policing communities
    that were already over-policed in the data
  - Healthcare algorithms: underestimating needs
    of Black patients based on cost data
  - Search engines: reinforcing stereotypes in
    image results and autocomplete suggestions
Key exam point: The algorithm itself isn't "racist" or "sexist," it's reflecting the bias in its training data or design. The responsibility lies with the humans who chose the data and designed the system. But the IMPACT on affected groups is real regardless of intent.

Quick Check: Bias

A loan approval algorithm is trained on 10 years of past lending decisions. Historically, applicants from certain zip codes were denied more often. What is the risk of using this algorithm?
The algorithm will learn to deny applicants from those zip codes at higher rates, perpetuating historical discrimination. If those zip codes correlate with race or income, the algorithm effectively discriminates based on those factors, even if race/income aren't explicitly in the data. This is called "proxy discrimination": zip code acts as a proxy for race/class. Solution: audit the algorithm for disparate impact across demographics, diversify training data, and include human oversight.

4. Crowdsourcing

Definition

Crowdsourcing uses contributions from a large number of people, often online, to accomplish a task, gather data, or fund a project.

Types & Examples

Knowledge sharing:
  Wikipedia (collaborative encyclopedia)
  Stack Overflow (community Q&A for programmers)
  Open-source software (Linux, Python)

Citizen science:
  Foldit (protein folding game for research)
  Galaxy Zoo (classifying galaxies from images)
  eBird (birdwatchers reporting sightings)

Crowdfunding:
  Kickstarter, GoFundMe, Indiegogo
  Individuals fund projects with small donations

Data collection:
  Waze (drivers reporting traffic/hazards)
  OpenStreetMap (community-built maps)
  reCAPTCHA (humans labeling images for AI)

Benefits & Limitations

Benefits

Diverse perspectives and expertise

Scales beyond what one person/org can do

Low cost per contributor

Rapid data collection

Limitations

Quality control is difficult

Contributions may be inaccurate or biased

Not representative of all populations

Can be manipulated (fake reviews, vandalism)

Quick Check: Crowdsourcing

Wikipedia is a crowdsourced encyclopedia. Give one benefit and one limitation of using crowdsourcing for this purpose.
Benefit: Massive scale. Millions of articles in hundreds of languages, covering far more topics than any traditional encyclopedia could. Updated in real-time as events happen. Limitation: Quality varies. Anyone can edit, so information may be inaccurate, biased, or vandalized. Popular topics get more attention and accuracy than niche ones. Editors may have their own biases about what's "neutral."

5. Legal & Ethical: Copyright, Creative Commons, Open Source

Copyright

What: Legal protection for original creative works.
       Automatically applies when you create something.
What it means: Others cannot copy, distribute, or modify
       your work without permission.
Examples: Music, software, books, images, code.
Duration: Creator's lifetime + 70 years (in the US).
Violation: Using copyrighted material without permission
       is infringement (illegal).

Creative Commons (CC)

What: A set of FREE licenses that let creators
       choose HOW their work can be shared.
Why:  Copyright is "all rights reserved."
       Creative Commons is "some rights reserved."

Common CC conditions:
  BY       = Give credit to the creator (attribution)
  SA       = Share-alike (derivatives use same license)
  NC       = Non-commercial use only
  ND       = No derivatives (can't modify)

Example: CC BY-NC means you can use it for free
if you give credit and don't make money from it.

Open Source

What: Software whose source code is publicly available.
       Anyone can view, modify, and distribute it.
Examples: Linux, Python, Firefox, Android (partially)
Benefit: Community improvement, transparency, free to use
NOT the same as "no copyright":
       Open source still has licenses (MIT, GPL, Apache)
       that set rules for how it can be used.
Exam distinction: Copyright = automatic, all rights reserved. Creative Commons = creator CHOOSES to share some rights. Open source = source code available, specific license terms apply. Public domain = no copyright, completely free to use (like very old works).

Quick Check: Legal

A student uses an image from Google for a school project. The image has a "CC BY-NC" license. Is this OK?
Yes, as long as: 1. They give credit (BY = attribution required) 2. It's for a school project, not for profit (NC = non-commercial) They would NOT be allowed to: - Use it in something they sell - Claim they created it - Use it without crediting the creator

6. PII & Data Privacy

PII (Personally Identifiable Information)

Definition: Any data that can identify a specific person.

Obvious PII:
  Full name, email, phone number, SSN,
  home address, date of birth, driver's license

Less obvious PII:
  IP address, GPS location, biometric data
  (fingerprints, face scans), browsing history,
  device ID, cookies

Why it matters: PII can be used for identity theft,
  stalking, discrimination, or surveillance
  if it falls into the wrong hands.

How PII Is Collected

  - Account sign-ups (name, email, password)
  - Location services (GPS, Wi-Fi triangulation)
  - Cookies and tracking pixels on websites
  - Social media posts and interactions
  - Purchases and financial transactions
  - IoT devices (smart speakers, fitness trackers)
  - Public records (voter rolls, property records)

Data Breaches

What: Unauthorized access to stored personal data.
Impact: Identity theft, financial loss, privacy violation.
Examples: Company databases hacked, exposing millions
       of users' names, emails, passwords, credit cards.
Why it happens: Weak security, unpatched software,
       phishing attacks on employees, insider threats.
Exam point: Even "anonymized" data can sometimes be re-identified by combining it with other datasets. True anonymity is very hard to achieve. The exam loves questions about the gap between claimed privacy and actual risk.

Quick Check: PII

Which of the following is PII? A. A country's population B. The average temperature today C. A user's IP address D. The number of cars on a highway
C. An IP address. An IP address can be traced to a specific device and location, potentially identifying an individual user. A, B, and D are aggregate/general data that don't identify a specific person.

7. Cybersecurity: Encryption, Phishing, Malware

Encryption

What: Converting readable data (plaintext) into
       unreadable scrambled data (ciphertext) using a key.
Why:  Protects data during transmission and storage.

Symmetric encryption:
  Same key to encrypt AND decrypt.
  Fast, but you need a secure way to share the key.
  Example: You and your friend both know the password.

Public key (asymmetric) encryption:
  TWO keys: a public key (shared openly) and a
  private key (kept secret by the owner).
  - Encrypt with PUBLIC key → only PRIVATE key can decrypt
  - Anyone can send you encrypted messages
  - Only YOU can read them (with your private key)
  
  Used in: HTTPS, email encryption, digital signatures

Phishing

What: A social engineering attack where an attacker
       sends fake messages pretending to be a
       trusted source (bank, school, employer).
Goal: Trick the victim into revealing passwords,
       credit card numbers, or clicking malicious links.
How to spot it:
  - Urgent language ("Your account will be deleted!")
  - Suspicious sender address (slight misspellings)
  - Generic greetings ("Dear Customer" not your name)
  - Links that don't match the claimed destination
Key point: Phishing targets HUMANS, not technology.
  The best firewall can't stop someone from
  voluntarily typing their password into a fake site.

Other Threats

Malware: Software designed to damage or gain
  unauthorized access. Includes viruses, worms,
  ransomware, spyware, trojans.

Keylogger: Records everything you type
  (passwords, messages, credit card numbers).

Ransomware: Encrypts your files and demands
  payment to unlock them.

DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service):
  Floods a server with so many requests that
  it can't serve legitimate users. Doesn't steal
  data, just takes the service offline.

Quick Check: Security

What is the difference between symmetric and public key encryption?
Symmetric: ONE key for both encrypt and decrypt. Both sender and receiver must have the same key. Problem: how do you securely share the key? Public key: TWO keys. Public key encrypts, private key decrypts. You can share your public key with everyone. Only your private key can decode messages encrypted with your public key. Solves the key-sharing problem.

8. Authentication & Cookies

Authentication

What: Verifying that a user is who they claim to be.

Three factors of authentication:
  1. Something you KNOW    (password, PIN, security question)
  2. Something you HAVE    (phone for SMS code, security key, badge)
  3. Something you ARE     (fingerprint, face scan, iris scan)

Multifactor authentication (MFA):
  Uses TWO or more factors from DIFFERENT categories.
  Example: Password (know) + SMS code (have)
  
  NOT multifactor: Password + security question
  (both are "something you know" = same category)
Exam trap: Two passwords or a password + security question is NOT multifactor because they're both "something you know." MFA requires factors from different categories.

Cookies & Digital Footprint

Cookies: Small data files websites store on your browser.
  - Track login sessions (stay signed in)
  - Remember preferences (language, dark mode)
  - Track browsing behavior for advertising
  - Can be used across sites (third-party cookies)

Digital footprint: The trail of data you leave online.
  Active: Things you intentionally post (social media, emails)
  Passive: Things collected without your direct action
           (cookies, IP logs, location tracking, metadata)

Key point: Your digital footprint is essentially permanent.
  Even deleted content may be cached, screenshotted,
  or stored on servers somewhere.

Quick Check: Authentication & Cookies

Which is TRUE multifactor authentication? A. Password + security question B. Fingerprint + face scan C. Password + SMS code sent to phone D. Two different passwords
C. Password (something you KNOW) + SMS code (something you HAVE, your phone). A is NOT MFA: both are "something you know." B is debatable but technically both are "something you are" (same category). D is NOT MFA: both are "something you know." True MFA combines DIFFERENT categories.
